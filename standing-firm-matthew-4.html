Standing Firm

THE TEMPTATION OF JESUS · MATTHEW 4:1–11

Central question: How do we recognize temptation, understand God’s Word in its proper context, and remain faithful when our desires or circumstances are used to challenge our trust in God? 1 | Historical setting Matthew opens his Gospel by presenting Jesus as the promised Messiah, the son of David and son of Abraham (Matthew 1:1). The Gospel is traditionally attributed to Matthew, one of the twelve disciples. Its repeated references to Israel’s Scriptures help readers see how Jesus fulfills God’s promises. The precise date and original audience are debated, but the book clearly expects readers to understand Israel’s history and covenant story. Before the wilderness account, John the Baptist called people to repentance and prepared the way for the coming kingdom (Matthew 3:1–12). Jesus then came to be baptized. At His baptism, the Spirit descended and the heavenly voice declared, “This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased” (Matthew 3:17). The very next scene begins with Jesus being led by the Spirit into the wilderness. The movement matters: the Father has identified Jesus as His Son before the tempter begins challenging what sonship means. The wilderness was not an accidental detour. In Israel’s history, the wilderness was the place where the people learned dependence on God after their deliverance from Egypt. Israel faced hunger, uncertainty, and repeated tests of trust. Jesus’ forty days invite comparison with Israel’s forty years, but Matthew does not say that the two experiences are identical. Jesus repeatedly answers Satan with words from Deuteronomy, where Moses reminded Israel what they had learned in the wilderness. What happened before the temptation? Matthew 1–2 establishes Jesus’ identity and early life. Matthew 3 introduces John’s call to repentance, Jesus’ baptism, and the Father’s declaration. Matthew 4 then shows Jesus meeting temptation before beginning His public proclamation (Matthew 4:17). The order helps us see that His identity as Son is given by the Father, not earned by performing for the tempter. QUESTION What did the Father say about Jesus before the wilderness testing began? THE STORY IN FIVE EVENTS: The Spirit leads Jesus into the wilderness; Jesus fasts and becomes hungry; the tempter challenges Him through three proposals; Jesus responds from Scripture; Satan departs and angels minister to Jesus. 3 | Examine Matthew 4:1–4 Verse 1: “led up of the Spirit” Matthew 4:1 (KJV) “Then was Jesus led up of the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted of the devil.” Matthew distinguishes the Spirit’s leading from the devil’s tempting. The Spirit leads Jesus into the setting; the devil is the one who tempts. The verse does not portray the Father as trying to make Jesus sin. James 1:13 teaches that God does not tempt anyone with evil. This distinction matters when studying temptation: facing a temptation is not itself the same as consenting to it. Jesus was tempted, yet He remained without sin (Hebrews 4:15). The wilderness is a real place of isolation and hardship. The account is not teaching that every difficult season comes from Satan or that every hardship means a believer is outside God’s will. Here, Jesus is precisely where the Spirit has led Him. The question is not merely where He is, but how He will respond. QUESTION Who is acting in this verse, and what does each one do? Verse 2: “he was afterward an hungred” Matthew 4:2 (KJV) “And when he had fasted forty days and forty nights, he was afterward an hungred.” Matthew plainly names Jesus’ hunger. Fasting means abstaining from food for a period; the passage does not explain every detail of the fast. Jesus’ bodily need is genuine, not pretend. Hunger is not sinful. The temptation will concern whether He uses His power at the tempter’s direction rather than living in faithful dependence on His Father. The forty- day period also calls to mind Moses’ forty days (Exodus 34:28) and Israel’s wilderness history, although each account has its own purpose. QUESTION Why does Matthew tell us that Jesus was hungry before recording Satan’s words? Verse 3: “If thou be the Son of God” Matthew 4:3 (KJV) “And when the tempter came to him, he said, If thou be the Son of God, command that these stones be made bread.” The tempter approaches at a moment of genuine weakness and directs attention toward Jesus’ identity. “If thou be the Son of God” follows the Father’s declaration at baptism. The wording can function as a challenge to demonstrate sonship; it does not require us to conclude that Satan was uncertain who Jesus was. The proposal sounds practical—Jesus needs food—but it asks Him to take His cue from the tempter. A legitimate need can become the setting for an illegitimate demand. Compare Genesis 3:1–6. There the serpent questions and reframes God’s command, and the woman considers the fruit’s attractiveness and usefulness. The accounts differ: Eve was in a garden with food available, while Jesus is hungry in the wilderness. Their shared concern is whether God’s word or the tempter’s suggestion will govern the response. Be careful not to claim Matthew explicitly says every temptation works in exactly the same way. QUESTION Is hunger the problem in this verse, or is the tempter’s proposal the problem? Explain from the text. Verse 4: “It is written” Matthew 4:4 (KJV) “But he answered and said, It is written, Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God.” Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 8:3. Moses reminded Israel that God allowed them to hunger and fed them with manna so they would learn that life depends on God’s word and provision, not food alone. Jesus does not deny the importance of bread. Rather, He refuses to treat immediate physical relief as more authoritative than His Father. The passage teaches dependence, not neglect of basic needs. Notice how Jesus uses Scripture: He does not pull out a sentence disconnected from its setting. His quotation speaks directly to hunger and trust in the wilderness. This models an important study habit: read a verse in its paragraph and in the larger biblical story before using it to justify a decision. QUESTION What is the difference between having a real need and allowing that need to govern obedience? 4 | Examine Matthew 4:5–7 Verses 5–6: “for it is written” Matthew 4:5–6 (KJV) “Then the devil taketh him up into the holy city, and setteth him on a pinnacle of the temple, And saith unto him, If thou be the Son of God, cast thyself down: for it is written, He shall give his angels charge concerning thee: and in their hands they shall bear thee up, lest at any time thou dash thy foot against a stone.” The “holy city” is Jerusalem; the temple was central to Israel’s worship. Matthew does not specify the exact architectural location of the pinnacle. Satan now quotes Scripture— Psalm 91:11–12—to urge Jesus to jump. This is a crucial turning point: knowing biblical words is not the same as handling them faithfully. The psalm celebrates God’s protection of those who dwell in His care; it is not an instruction to manufacture danger in order to demand a rescue. In the KJV, Psalm 91:11 includes the phrase “to keep thee in all thy ways,” which is not included in Satan’s quotation as Matthew reports it. The omission is worth observing, but the larger problem is the proposed use of the passage. Even an accurately quoted verse can be misapplied when it is detached from its purpose. The lesson is not that believers should fear Scripture, but that Scripture must be read in context. QUESTION What is Satan asking Jesus to prove? QUESTION Why is quoting a Bible verse not enough to establish that an action is wise or faithful? Verse 7: “It is written again” Matthew 4:7 (KJV) “Jesus said unto him, It is written again, Thou shalt not tempt the Lord thy God.” Jesus answers from Deuteronomy 6:16, where Israel is told not to test the Lord as they did at Massah (see Exodus 17:1–7). At Massah the people demanded proof of God’s presence amid their need for water. Jesus refuses to force a dramatic demonstration of divine protection. Trusting God is different from creating a reckless test and then insisting God intervene. Jesus’ words also teach how to interpret Scripture with Scripture. Psalm 91 cannot rightly be used to cancel the command not to test God. When one reading of a verse seems to require disobedience to another clear teaching, examine the context and reconsider the proposed interpretation. QUESTION What is the difference between asking God for help in danger and creating danger to demand proof? 5 | Examine Matthew 4:8–11 Verses 8–9: “fall down and worship me” Matthew 4:8–9 (KJV) “Again, the devil taketh him up into an exceeding high mountain, and sheweth him all the kingdoms of the world, and the glory of them; And saith unto him, All these things will I give thee, if thou wilt fall down and worship me.” The third proposal makes the issue of allegiance unmistakable. Satan offers kingdoms and their glory in exchange for worship. Matthew does not explain how the kingdoms were shown or ask readers to settle every question about the extent of Satan’s authority. The point of the narrative is the price attached to the offer: worship that belongs to God alone. A promised benefit does not make the means acceptable. In the wider Gospel, Jesus is already the promised King (Matthew 2:2), and later He declares that all authority in heaven and earth has been given to Him (Matthew 28:18). The temptation therefore presents a false shortcut: glory without faithful obedience. Notice how temptation may offer something attractive while concealing the cost of misplaced worship. QUESTION What does Satan offer, and what does he require in return? Verse 10: “him only shalt thou serve” Matthew 4:10 (KJV) “Then saith Jesus unto him, Get thee hence, Satan: for it is written, Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve.” Jesus cites Deuteronomy 6:13, part of Moses’ warning that Israel must not forget the Lord or follow other gods when they enter the land. Worship is not merely a religious song or outward gesture; in this setting it concerns exclusive loyalty and service. Jesus refuses to exchange faithfulness for the appearance of immediate success. His firm command, “Get thee hence,” rejects both the proposal and the tempter’s claim on His allegiance. This passage does not mean that all desires for achievement, provision, or influence are sinful. It asks what we are willing to worship or disobey to obtain them. Jesus’ response places God above every promised reward. QUESTION Why is worship the decisive issue in the third temptation? Verse 11: “angels came and ministered unto him” Matthew 4:11 (KJV) “Then the devil leaveth him, and, behold, angels came and ministered unto him.” Matthew ends the scene with Satan departing and angels ministering to Jesus. The verb “ministered” means they served or attended to Him; the verse does not specify all that their service involved. The ending confirms that Jesus did not need to obey the tempter to receive the Father’s care. Notice also that the passage describes this particular confrontation; it does not promise that every believer’s temptation will end immediately or that every trial will resolve visibly. QUESTION What does the ending show about Jesus’ faithfulness and God’s care? 6 | Supporting Scripture: reading and obeying God’s Word Genesis 3:1–6 — questioning and reframing God’s word The serpent begins with a question about what God said. Eve’s response includes “neither shall ye touch it” and “lest ye die” (Genesis 3:3), whereas the command as recorded in Genesis 2:16–17 does not include the touching prohibition and says “thou shalt surely die.” Genesis does not tell us who introduced the additional phrase, so avoid claiming that Eve intentionally changed the command or that this alone caused the fall. The comparison invites careful listening: what did God actually say, what did another speaker say, and what conclusions did the hearer draw? QUESTION Which words are in Genesis 2:16–17, and which appear in Genesis 3:3? What can we know—and not know—from that comparison? Deuteronomy 8:2–3 — dependence learned in the wilderness Moses explains that Israel’s wilderness experience exposed what was in their hearts and taught them dependence on God. Jesus quotes verse 3 in His first response. Read the original passage before applying it: it concerns God’s provision of manna, His instruction, and Israel’s need to remember Him. Psalm 91:11–12 and Deuteronomy 6:16 — promise and presumption Psalm 91 expresses confidence in God’s care. Deuteronomy 6:16 forbids testing Him. Read together, they show why a promise of protection should not be turned into permission to manufacture a crisis. Context helps us distinguish trusting a promise from demanding a spectacle. Hebrews 4:15 and James 1:13–15 — temptation and sin Hebrews 4:15 says Jesus was tempted yet without sin, giving believers confidence that He understands weakness. James 1 distinguishes God from the source of evil temptation and traces the danger of desire when it is embraced and allowed to grow. These passages help beginners distinguish experiencing pressure from choosing disobedience. Ephesians 6:17 and Psalm 119:11 — the Word and standing firm Paul calls the word of God the sword of the Spirit (Ephesians 6:17). The psalmist speaks of hiding God’s word in the heart (Psalm 119:11). Matthew 4 illustrates a faithful response rooted in Scripture understood and obeyed, not a mechanical formula in which repeating a verse guarantees that temptation disappears. QUESTION What is one way to test whether a Bible verse is being used in its proper context? 7 | Closing: Standing Firm in the Word The wilderness account begins with the Father’s declaration of Jesus as His beloved Son and then shows how Jesus responds when His identity, physical hunger, and allegiance are challenged. Satan offers bread, a dramatic display of protection, and worldly glory. Jesus responds with Scripture from Deuteronomy, keeping God’s word and worship at the center of His choices. Matthew does not present Jesus merely as an example of willpower; he reveals the faithful Son who succeeds where God’s people so often failed. The lesson is not to memorize isolated verses as magic words. It is to know God’s character, read His Word carefully, and allow its meaning to guide decisions. We can acknowledge genuine needs without allowing them to rule us, refuse to twist promises into demands, and recognize that a desirable outcome never justifies misplaced worship. The text points us first to who Jesus is, and then to what it means to follow Him. QUESTION Which temptation most clearly shows the danger of using a true statement in the wrong way? FINAL REFLECTION Return to Matthew 4:4, 4:7, and 4:10. What does each answer reveal about Jesus’ trust, His understanding of Scripture, and His loyalty to the Father? KEY TAKEAWAY Standing firm means more than being able to quote Scripture. It means knowing what God has said, understanding His Word in context, and remaining faithful to Him when temptation offers another way.
